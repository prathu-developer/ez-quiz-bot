Currency conundrum: On the BRICS New Delhi Declaration

“ India navigates strategic currency trade choices within BRICS amidst geopolitical and economic risks.
September 17, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The BRICS New Delhi Declaration shows that progress in enhancing local currency trade has been incremental2. The paragraph pertaining to3 local currency trade acknowledges the efforts of various task forces and committees but falls short of4 offering any concrete proposals. Recently, the Commerce Ministry indicated that India’s rupee trade with its BRICS partners was limited, with only the UAE and Russia engaged in it. Even those volumes are relatively small. Until recently, Russia had struggled to dispose of5 the rupees it was accumulating6 from its exports to India. Some avenues have now opened, with Russia being forced to import petroleum products from India due to Ukraine’s attacks. But that is a trickle7. Another option is to treat any BRICS currency as ‘local’. India has already been using the UAE Dirham to pay for Russian oil. However, the latest Declaration’s mention of promoting BRICS local currency trade “while respecting national priorities and acknowledging that there is no one-size-fits-all8 approach” is telling9. Clearly, there are some intra-BRICS issues that need to be addressed. The deliberations10 are not public, but it would not be surprising if it was India that had emphasised national priorities and raised problems with a “one-size-fits-all8” approach. Local currency trade involves several strategic considerations for India.

On the one hand12, India would prefer to continue being paid for its exports in dollars. A depreciating11 rupee ensures that every dollar paid to India results in a higher amount of rupees. As a country looking to boost exports, India would want to retain this advantage. On the other hand, India is also a major importer and would like to pay in relatively cheaper local currencies. It will eventually have to choose. Then there is the fact that China accounts for about two-thirds of all BRICS exports. BRICS local currency trade will largely be trade in the yuan. Relations with China might be thawing13 now, but India would still be loath to14 conduct its business in the yuan. It is also important to distinguish between15 local currency trade and a BRICS currency. While India has so far been cautiously supportive of16 the former, it has been vocal in opposing a BRICS currency, largely because of China’s likely dominance of such a currency. Then there is U.S. President Donald Trump’s threat of 100% tariffs on countries adopting a BRICS currency. India has displayed a pragmatic17 approach to dealing with Mr. Trump, and it will not court18 such tariff threats lightly. Countries such as Iran and Russia have pressing19 reasons to move away from20 the dollar. So far, India does not have a convincing enough one, and the New Delhi Declaration reflects that.

Hog in the limelight: On Assam’s pygmy hog programme

“ Protecting the endangered pygmy hog demands restoring grassland habitats alongside successful captive breeding efforts.
September 17, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The decline of the pygmy hog — the sole surviving species of the genus Porcula and the world’s smallest wild suid — is a narrative about the decline of India’s alluvial2 floodplains. The pygmy hog depends significantly on dense grasslands to feed, conceal itself, and reproduce, so distress among these animals is an indicator of the floodplain suffering degradation3. Protecting the pygmy hog can also extend protections to threatened species such as the Bengal florican, hispid hare, hog deer, and greater one-horned rhinoceros. The Assam government was recently right to laud4 the efforts of a captive breeding5 programme that increased the pygmy hog population in the State 32-fold in the last three decades. Breeding from a small founder stock6 while preventing diseases — suids are highly susceptible to7 swine diseases — and inbreeding8 requires conservationists to track pedigree9, follow biosafety10 protocols, and condition individuals before release. That said, the species’ survival in the wild depends on the survival of specific ecosystems, and the latter remains a work in progress11. The pygmy hog was believed to be extinct12 by the mid-20th century as floodplains were converted for farms, tea plantations, and flood control infrastructure and were degraded by invasive13 plants and altered flood cycles, with the unscientific burning of grasslands further fragmenting14 habitats. A few individuals were rediscovered in 1971, prompting15 one conservation effort. The current efforts are due to16 the Pygmy Hog Conservation Programme from 1995.

Even now, the difficulty of spotting and counting these animals means that it is hard to say how many exist in the wild; per the Durrell Wildlife Conservation Trust, there were some 250 individuals in early 2025, with the last surviving natural population in the Panbari grasslands area of the Manas National Park. Since they continue to be threatened, the captive individuals form the insurance group, but even so, the group’s long-term evolutionary fitness17 is still not guaranteed. Researchers are also alert to18 the accumulation of harmful gene variants in the population and low genetic diversity, and thus look forward to19 the pygmy hog’s natural habitats being restored. Alluvial2 floodplains are dynamic20 ecosystems; in addition to threats such as land conversion, efforts to ‘save’ them, such as widespread dry-season fires, can still destroy the dense cover the pygmy hog needs. On the other hand, suppressing21 ecological processes can leave trees and shrubs to turn grassland into woodland. Given the plan to increase the wild pygmy hog population to 300 by 2040, Assam needs to revert22 the fragmentation of its protected grasslands, especially in Rupahi and Kanchanbari, and to restore buffer zones23 around the Manas and Orang National Parks and the Sonai Rupai Wildlife Sanctuary.

Students need a safe home. That’s Delhi University’s duty

“ Urgent policy intervention and administrative synergy are vital to secure safe housing for university students.
September 17, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The Satya Niketan tragedy has brought much-needed attention to accommodation for Delhi University (DU) students. The figures underline1 the scale of the problem: Around 70,000 students are enrolled in DU’s undergraduate programmes every year, and its PG courses have more than 10,000 seats. While the university’s hostels have 3,422 seats, the colleges affiliated2 to it have 3,788 seats. Many of these students come from outside the city, and it’s clear that DU’s hostel network cannot accommodate them. The result is a vast dependence on3 private paying-guest facilities and rented accommodation, often in densely packed neighbourhoods with unsafe conditions close to university campuses. For outstation students, the soaring accommodation costs offset4 DU’s affordable tuition fees. Seven months after assuming office5, the university’s vice chancellor announced plans to build two 1,000-bed hostels near North Campus. But as an investigation in this paper shows, as the VC begins a second term next month. the projects remain held up6 by funding constraints7, space crunch, land-use restrictions and administrative hurdles8.

The immediate priority must be to expand safe and affordable hostel accommodation, while also recognising that the gap is too large to be bridged9 by construction projects initiated by the university alone. In the past, the Delhi government, DU and its colleges have rarely come together to devise10 solutions that treat student housing as part of the university’s essential infrastructure. Delhi’s master plans have allowed property owners to adapt their houses to provide student accommodation, provided they adhere to11 safety norms. In practice, these caveats12 were flouted13. At the same time, DU hasn’t shown much initiative to engage with14 the private accommodation market around its campuses. Options such as a university-maintained database of accommodation, with information on rents, occupancy, and compliance with15 basic safety standards, were never explored.

After the Satya Niketan tragedy, Delhi’s Chief Minister has called for a survey to identify colleges with sufficient space for hostel construction. These units will be built via public-private partnership16, and the Delhi CM has promised that the government will ensure students’ safety. These are steps in the right direction17, but the government needs to do more. For starters, it needs to nudge18 the colleges to look at out-of-the-box19 solutions — drawing in entrepreneurs by pooling the colleges’ unused parcels of land into hostel sites could be an option. A student life in Delhi helps many youngsters spread their wings20. As the brother of one of the victims of the Satya Niketan incident told this paper, “he loved the city”. The best tribute to those who lost their lives last week would be to end the lack of imagination and political will21 that denies people like them a safe home in the city.

For a small fee, UPI has become stronger

“ Reintroducing a modest merchant discount rate ensures long-term financial sustainability and technological resilience for UPI.
September 17, 2026 · Daily Editorial Reader · 2 min read

The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) has announced that UPI transactions over Rs 2,000 will attract1 a fee of 0.4 per cent from October 15. Called the Merchant Discount Rate (MDR), the fee, capped at2 Rs 300 per transaction, applies only to person-to-merchant (P2M) transactions. The Union Ministry of Finance has stated that banks have been “advised4” to ensure merchants — e-commerce websites, grocery shops or shopkeepers, receiving more than Rs 1 lakh per month from customers paying through UPI — do not pass on5 the cost to customers. It has also been clarified6 that for certain categories of transactions — such as UPI payments received for rail tickets, fuel and agricultural inputs7, credit card dues, telecom or utility bills, insurance premiums8, and tax payments — a flat merchant fee of Rs 5 will be charged from merchants. There are several reasons why this decision should be welcomed.

These changes follow the passage9 of the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Act, 2026 last month, which built on10 the recommendations11 of the 32nd report of the Standing Committee on Finance. The report had noted that “the absence of MDR makes the UPI ecosystem12 financially unsustainable13”. The MDR is the fee paid to the bank or payment service provider14 for processing digital transactions, and crores of transactions take place each year. In 2025-26, for instance, there were more than 24,000 crore UPI transactions, and their total value was Rs 314 lakh crore. UPI was introduced in 2016 with an MDR. The zero-MDR policy was adopted in 2020 to expand the adoption15 of digital transactions. The balanced approach in the latest shift will ensure a sustainable16 revenue flow to invest, while also enabling seamless17 digital payments for a large section of people — this could lead to18 greater reach.

The decision has attracted some criticism19. The main Opposition party, for instance, has claimed the move will hurt consumers, especially the poor ones. Such criticism19 is unfounded20. More than 95 per cent of UPI transactions are below the Rs 2,000 threshold21. These changes do not apply to person-to-person transactions, and critical public services will have a flat fee. There is an MDR on credit cards as well. Allowing these charges will ensure more competition in the marketplace22 and better service delivery.
